## Fold 2 — The name

This is the fold people screenshot. It gets room — big type, nothing else
competing, generous space above and below.

> ## In Hindi, calling someone a <span class="hi" data-en="chameleon">गिरगिट</span> is not a compliment.
>
> *Girgit ki tarah <span class="hi" data-en="to change colour">रंग बदलना</span>* — to change colour like a chameleon.
> It's what you call a person who won't stay the same.
>
> ### We took the name anyway.
>
> Because changing colour isn't dishonesty. It's attention.
> A chameleon set against bark reads as bark. It belongs to where it is.
>
> That's the whole job. The work should belong to you — carry your
> <span class="hi" data-en="temperament">मिज़ाज</span>, your market, your
> ambition. Not our portfolio.
>
> Look at a site we built and you should see your company. Not us.

**Notes.**

Three Hindi words is one over the per-page budget, but this fold is the
exception and the reason for the rule — they are the subject of the paragraph,
not garnish. The rest of the page then gets **one**, in Fold 6. Nine folds, four
Hindi words total.

Load-bearing check: गिरगिट is followed immediately by "chameleon" in the next
line. रंग बदलना is translated in the same sentence. मिज़ाज sits in a list of
three where the other two carry the meaning. Delete all three and every sentence
still reads. Good.

**On the biology.** "Set against bark reads as bark" is the popular
understanding; chameleons actually shift colour mostly for temperature and
social signalling, and camouflage is secondary. It's metaphor and it reads as
metaphor — but if you'd rather not stake a line on it, swap to: *"A chameleon
answers to where it is. Nothing about it is fixed except the attention."*

## Fold 3 — Work

Four to six projects. Wolff Olins discipline: project name, one line, tags.
No paragraph, no "the challenge / the solution" on the homepage.

> ## Recent work

| Project | Line | Tags |
|---|---|---|
| `[[CLIENT 1]]` | `[[One line — what changed for them, not what we made]]` | `[[Brand · Web · 7 days]]` |
| `[[CLIENT 2]]` | `[[…]]` | `[[…]]` |
| `[[CLIENT 3]]` | `[[…]]` | `[[…]]` |
| `[[CLIENT 4]]` | `[[…]]` | `[[…]]` |

> → See all work

**Notes for filling these in.** The one-liner describes the *outcome for the
client*, never the deliverable. Wolff Olins writes "Adding a spark to the coffee
run," not "brand identity and packaging system." If a project can only be
described by its deliverables, it isn't ready to be a homepage tile — put it on
the Work page instead.

**If a client is under NDA**, do what Litmus does and say so plainly — they run
a Coca-Cola entry that ends "we might have featured this project with more
details if we weren't under an NDA." It reads as credible, not evasive.

**Four is the minimum for this fold to work.** With three or fewer, cut the grid
and run one project full-bleed instead — a single strong case study beats a
sparse grid every time.

## Fold 4 — The seven-day offer

> ## Seven days, from approved scope to live.
>
> Not a rough first draft. A website you would be happy to send to an investor
> on day eight.
>
> The clock doesn't start when you email us. It starts when the groundwork is
> done — content collected, references studied, sitemap agreed, visual direction
> set, scope signed off. Then seven days.
>
> **Day 1** — direction, on screen, not in a deck.
> **Day 3** — the real thing, in a browser, on your phone.
> **Day 7** — live.
>
> One revision round is included. If the project is genuinely bigger than seven
> days, we say so before you pay anything, ship a complete V1 in seven, and take
> the rest into Phase 2.
>
> → How the seven days work

**Notes.** The pre-production gate is the most trust-building thing in the whole
operating doc and most agencies would bury it. Put it in the second sentence.
It's the difference between a promise and a stunt, and it qualifies out the
clients who'd break the promise anyway.

## Fold 5 — Why the speed is real

Three columns. This fold exists because unexplained speed reads as *cheap* or
*template*, and one of those kills the premium positioning.

> ## Fast isn't a discount.

> **A small senior team**
> Three or four people, all senior, one owner per project. Nothing waits in a
> queue behind someone else's approval.

> **No handoff**
> The designer builds the frontend. Nothing is lost translating a file into
> code, because there's no translation — the person who drew it ships it.

> **The slow part happens first**
> Research, references, sitemap, direction and scope are all settled before day
> one. Seven days of building, not seven days of deciding.

**Notes.** All three are true and none of them says AI. Per the operating doc,
AI stays a production advantage demonstrated through the work, not a claim on
the homepage.

## Fold 7 — Who this is for

Qualifying copy. It should politely repel the wrong client — that's the job.

> ## We work best with
>
> Founders who need an idea to exist by a date.
> Businesses whose digital presence is a decade behind the company.
> Agencies who need real design capacity, quietly.
> Creators building something bigger than a profile.
>
> ## Less well with
>
> Anyone shopping purely on price.
> Anyone who wants to art-direct the art director.
>
> We'll recommend the direction and argue for it. You approve the big calls. If
> a decision would visibly damage the work, we'll say so, explain why, and
> propose something better — that argument is part of what you're paying for.

**Notes.** The "less well with" block is the highest-leverage copy on the page.
It costs you the clients who'd be unprofitable and makes the ones who stay trust
you more. Litmus does the same thing with "We're creative. You're not creative.
What next?"
