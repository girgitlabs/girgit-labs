# Work

You have real projects — this page is built to receive them. Everything here is
either final copy or a fill-in template with the writing rules attached, so the
case studies come out consistent no matter who writes them.

---

## Index page

> **Eyebrow** — Selected work

> # Different every time. That's the point.

> If these looked like they came from the same studio, we'd be doing it wrong.

Then the grid. Project name, one line, tags, image. Nothing else.

> **Filters** — All · Web · Product & apps · Branding · Video · AI experiences

**Notes.** The headline does real work: it pre-empts the objection a visitor
forms while scrolling a varied portfolio — *"is there a point of view here?"* —
and converts inconsistency into the thesis. This is the payoff of the Fold 2
name story on the homepage. Without that fold, this line doesn't land; with it,
it's the second half of an argument.

**Ordering.** Strongest first, always. Not chronological. Nobody scrolls to the
bottom of a work page looking for your best project.

**Minimum viable page.** Six projects makes a page. Three makes a page that
looks like a page with three projects on it. If you have fewer than six, drop
the grid and run them full-width, stacked, one screen each — fewer, bigger,
reads as confidence rather than thin.

## Case study template

Every case study uses these six blocks in this order. Resist adding a seventh.

### Block 1 — Header

> `[[CLIENT NAME]]`
> # `[[One line. What changed for them.]]`
>
> `[[Sector]]` · `[[Services]]` · `[[Year]]` · `[[Duration]]`

**Writing the line.** It describes the outcome, not the deliverable.

- Wolff Olins: *"Adding a spark to the coffee run."*
- Sparklin: *"Rebuilding internet banking from the ground up."*
- Not: *"Brand identity, website and packaging system."*

If the only honest line is a list of deliverables, the project isn't a case
study yet — it's a portfolio image. Put it in the grid without a detail page.

### Block 2 — The situation

Two or three sentences. What was true before, and why it was a problem.

> `[[Who they are, in one sentence a stranger would understand.]]`
> `[[What was broken, costing them, or missing.]]`

**Rule.** Never make the client look stupid. The situation is a context, not a
diagnosis of their incompetence — future clients read this and imagine
themselves in it.

### Block 3 — What we did

Three to five sentences. Decisions, not tasks.

> `[[The central decision that shaped everything else.]]`
> `[[One or two things that decision made possible.]]`
> `[[Anything genuinely hard, and how it was solved.]]`

**Rule.** "We redesigned the navigation" is a task. "We cut the navigation from
nine items to four, because their analytics showed six of them had never been
clicked" is a decision. Only decisions belong here.

**Rule.** If it was a seven-day build, say the number. It is the single most
memorable fact you have and it belongs in the body, not just a tag.

### Block 4 — The work

Images. Minimal captions, and only where a caption adds something the image
can't. No "final logo design." No "mobile view."

**Rule.** If there is video — and with Harsh on the team there should be — this
is where it goes. A ten-second scroll-through of the built site does more than
six static screens.

### Block 5 — Result

> `[[Metric]]` · `[[Metric]]` · `[[Quote]]`

**Rule, and this is the one that matters.** A weak metric is worse than none.
"Increased engagement" means nothing and reads as a cover for having no numbers.
If you have real figures — load time, conversion, bounce, signups, time to
launch — use them. If you don't, use a client quote and nothing else, or ask
the client for one number three months after launch. Build that ask into your
project close; it costs one email and it's what makes the next case study
credible.

**Do not invent numbers.** One fabricated stat that a prospect checks costs you
more than every case study on the site earns.

### Block 6 — Next

> → Next project: `[[NAME]]`
> → Start a project

## Worked example

For calibration — this is the standard, written against a project shape you
already have.

> **Sha Juharmal Phoolchand**
> # A furniture house since 1942, finally visible online.
>
> Sirohi, Rajasthan · Brand, web design, frontend · 2026 · 7 days
>
> **The situation**
> Four generations of furniture-making in Rajasthan, a showroom people drive
> from three districts to visit, and a web presence that was a directory
> listing with a phone number. Every enquiry started with someone explaining
> what the business was.
>
> **What we did**
> The catalogue was the whole argument, so we built the site around it rather
> than around a homepage — 28 pieces, filterable, with the count derived from
> the page itself so it can never go stale. One typeface, black on white,
> hairline rules: the restraint keeps the furniture the loudest thing on the
> screen. Photography wasn't ready in week one, so we shipped with commissioned
> line-art built to be swapped for real images without touching the layout.
> Live in seven days.
>
> **Result**
> `[[Enquiries per month, before and after]]`
> `[[Client quote]]`

**Notes.** Note what the example does — the constraint (no photography) is
stated as a solved problem rather than hidden. Prospects trust a studio that
admits a project had constraints far more than one where everything went
perfectly.

Note also: this is the demo build in `C:\Personal projects\jawaharmal Phoolchand`,
and its own README says the collection names, timeline and projects are
invented. **Do not publish it as a client case study until it's a real
engagement with real content and the client's permission.** If it stays a
self-initiated piece, label it as one — "self-initiated" is honest and still
demonstrates the seven-day capability.

## NDA projects

> `[[CLIENT SECTOR]]`
> # `[[What changed]]`
>
> We can't name this one or show the work. Here's what we can say:
> `[[Two sentences about the problem and the approach.]]`

**Notes.** Litmus does exactly this on their Coca-Cola project and it reads as
credible rather than evasive. An NDA entry is worth more than an empty grid
slot — just never more than one or two, or it starts to look like a way of
having no portfolio.
